Subject: The test code failed as often as the code it tested. Is that known?


Dear Professor ——,

I have a number I can't interpret and no colleagues to ask.

I build a rule-based physical model — planetary formation, nuclear
binding energies, atmospheric radiative transfer, prebiotic chemistry.
107 modules, 406 self-checks, 47 published numeric results; it's the
third iteration of the same system. The governing rule is that every
result must carry a check that would catch it being wrong.

Working that way produced something I didn't set out to make: 25
faults, each annotated in source with its cause AND with the mechanism
that caught it.

    deriving something previously asserted        7
    faults in the verification machinery itself   7
    an automated check fired                      6
    comparison against an external known value    4
    cross-referencing an earlier release          1

Two things in that distribution I can't account for.

TESTS CAUGHT 6 OF 25. The largest category needed someone to re-derive
a quantity that had been typed in by hand. No test finds those — the
code was self-consistent and every check passed.

THE VERIFICATION CODE FAILED AT ABOUT THE RATE OF THE CODE IT
VERIFIES, 7 against 18. I have not found that quantified anywhere, and
I would not have guessed parity.

For texture, one entry: a rule forbidding duplicate constant
definitions caught its own author twice, within minutes of being
written.

The reason any of it is checkable from outside is that the model is
built downward to elements, and their properties are derived rather
than looked up. Valence comes from shell filling — carbon has four
outer electrons, so it bonds four ways — which reproduced all ten
hand-typed valences and produced fifty. Sixty-eight d-block elements
are REFUSED rather than assigned, with the reason stated: iron is +2
and +3 and deciding needs orbital energies the system doesn't compute.
Tissue is carried as stoichiometry, so growth withdraws specific atoms
from a finite pool and death returns them. 35% of rules bottom out on
that floor, and the periodic table is the most depended-on object in
the codebase — 254 rules, ahead of Boltzmann and Avogadro.

That is what lets a chemist check a claim without reading the code.
"A brain holds six times the phosphorus of ordinary tissue per atom"
is verifiable from atomic weights and two formulae. The same sentence
backed by a constant named BRAIN_PHOSPHORUS_FACTOR = 6.0 is verifiable
by nobody — and seven of the 25 faults were exactly that.

The data exists because of a convention rather than a tool. When a
check's premise turns out false, the check is flipped and kept with
its history instead of deleted — nine of those so far. Deleting is the
normal move, and it would have erased nine data points.

My question is simple: does a fault corpus annotated by
catching-mechanism already exist? Defects4J and BugSwarm are mined
from version control, which gives the fix but rarely what surfaced it.

I'm an independent developer with no university affiliation. The
corpus and a six-page write-up are attached — the corpus is the part
worth your time, and I'd be glad to be told this is a solved problem.

Trenten Bryant
